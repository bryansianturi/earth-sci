Two neighbouring plates can only do three things relative to each other: **move apart**, **move together**, or **slide sideways**. Each gives its name to one boundary type.

{{diagram:boundary-types}}

## Divergent boundaries: plates move apart

Where plates separate, hot mantle rock rises to fill the gap. As it rises and the pressure drops, it partly melts (decompression melting). The magma erupts or freezes, creating **new crust**.

- **On the sea floor** this makes a **mid-ocean ridge**: a mountain chain, about 65,000 km long, mostly hidden under water. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at around 2–3 cm per year in total; the East Pacific Rise spreads up to about 15 cm per year.
- **On land** a divergent boundary is a **rift valley**, like the East African Rift, where the continent is slowly splitting. Iceland sits where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level.

## Convergent boundaries: plates move together

When plates collide, the denser one usually sinks beneath the other in a process called **subduction**. What happens depends on what is colliding:

- **Ocean–ocean.** The older, colder, denser plate dives under the other. A deep **trench** forms (the Mariana Trench reaches about 10.9 km deep) and a curved chain of volcanic islands, an **island arc**, grows on the overriding plate. Examples: Japan's southern islands, the Aleutians, the Marianas.
- **Ocean–continent.** The dense ocean plate sinks under the lighter continent, producing a trench offshore and a **volcanic mountain chain** on the continent: the Andes (where the Nazca plate dives under South America) and the Cascades (Juan de Fuca plate under North America).
- **Continent–continent.** Continental crust is too light to sink far. Instead, the two continents crumple, fold and stack up, forming huge mountain belts and thick crust. The **Himalaya** and Tibetan Plateau formed (and are still rising) as India has pushed into Eurasia for about 50 million years. Volcanoes are largely absent because there is no sinking slab producing magma.

## Transform boundaries: plates slide past

At a transform boundary, plates grind sideways along a **strike-slip fault**. Crust is neither created nor destroyed. Most transforms are short faults on the sea floor that connect offset pieces of mid-ocean ridge. A few cut through continents, such as the **San Andreas Fault** in California, the **North Anatolian Fault** in Turkey and the **Alpine Fault** in New Zealand. The plates catch and lock, strain builds up for decades or centuries, and then the fault slips in an earthquake.

> **Common misconception.** "Crust is created at ridges, so the Earth must be getting bigger." It is not. New crust at ridges is balanced by old crust consumed at subduction zones, so the total surface area stays the same (over the timescale of human history, and to within very small limits for geologic history).

> **Common misconception.** "Boundaries are neat lines." Many are broad, complex zones, and some plates are subdividing or changing. The three-type model is a useful simplification, and real boundaries are often a mixture (for example, plates colliding at an angle both converge and slide sideways).

<summary>Go deeper: why do ocean plates sink and continents do not?</summary>

It comes down to density. Oceanic crust is basalt, about 2.9 g/cm³, but the key point is that the whole oceanic lithosphere, crust plus the cold mantle rock beneath, gets *denser* as it ages and cools. After about 30–50 million years it is on average denser than the hot asthenosphere below it, so it is ready to sink. Continental crust is made of lighter rock (about 2.7 g/cm³ on average) and is much thicker, so it remains buoyant and is rarely subducted deeply. This is why the oldest ocean floor is only about 180 million years old, while continents contain rocks up to around 4 billion years old.

At convergent boundaries volcanoes do not form directly from melting of the sinking plate. The sinking slab carries water locked in minerals. At around 100 km depth, heat and pressure squeeze the water out into the overlying mantle wedge, which **lowers its melting point** ("flux melting"). That magma, lighter than the surrounding rock, rises to feed the volcanic arc. It is why arcs sit a fairly consistent distance (often 100–200 km) behind the trench.

The timing of the continent–continent stage in the simulation shows slab break-off: the dense oceanic slab, no longer being pulled along with a continent, may detach and sink into the deep mantle. Whether and when this happens in a real collision (including the Himalaya) is an active research question, so treat that stage as one plausible model rather than settled fact.
