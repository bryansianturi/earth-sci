A **tectonic plate** is a large slab of lithosphere, the rigid outer shell of the Earth you met in [Earth's layers](../earths-layers/). The lithosphere is not one unbroken shell like an eggshell around a yolk. It is broken into pieces that fit together like a cracked and slowly shifting jigsaw puzzle.

## The plates

There are usually counted **seven or eight major plates** (Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, South American, and the Indo-Australian plate, which some maps split in two), plus a number of smaller ones such as the Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Philippine and Arabian plates. The exact count of small plates depends on how finely you slice them, so numbers you see in different books will differ.

Some key facts:

- Plates are about **100 km thick** on average, but thousands of kilometres wide.
- Most plates carry **both continents and ocean floor**. The North American plate, for example, extends from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to California. Continents do not "plough through" the ocean floor; they ride along with the rock beside them.
- Plates move at roughly **2–10 cm per year**, about as fast as fingernails grow. That is slow, but over 100 million years it adds up to thousands of kilometres.
- The plates meet at **boundaries**. Almost all the action (earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain-building) happens there. See [plate boundaries](../plate-boundaries/).

## Why can plates move?

Plates move because the rock under them is hot enough to flow slowly. The asthenosphere is weak, so it allows the lithosphere above to slide. Meanwhile the whole mantle slowly **convects**: hot, buoyant rock rises, cools near the surface, becomes denser and sinks again, a cycle taking tens of millions of years.

The plates are not passengers on top of this circulation. They are the **cold, stiff upper layer of the convection system itself**. Where a plate cools and sinks back into the mantle, that sinking is part of the convection.

## What pushes and pulls them?

{{diagram:plate-forces}}

Three forces are usually discussed:

1. **Slab pull.** At a subduction zone, old ocean floor is cold and dense, denser than the hot mantle below. It sinks, and its weight pulls the rest of the plate along behind it. Studies suggest this is the **largest** driving force for many plates. It fits an observation: plates attached to long sinking slabs, like the Pacific, tend to move fastest.
2. **Ridge push.** Near a mid-ocean ridge the sea floor is hot and elevated, so gravity makes it slide gently down and away from the ridge. This force is real but smaller than slab pull.
3. **Mantle drag.** Flowing mantle may drag the base of the plate along or resist its motion, depending on the direction. Its role varies between plates.

> **Common misconception.** "Plates float on a sea of molten magma." They do not. The asthenosphere is hot, weak rock that is nearly all **solid**, perhaps with a few percent melt in places. It flows only the way a glacier or very stiff putty does.

> **Common misconception.** "Convection currents act like conveyor belts that drag the plates around." The truth is more subtle: plates and mantle move together, and the plate's own cold edge sinking (slab pull) seems to do most of the driving.

<summary>Go deeper: measuring motion with GPS</summary>

Today we do not have to infer plate motion from old rocks; we can watch it. Networks of GPS receivers fixed to bedrock record their position to within millimetres. Over a few years the stations on different plates drift in different directions. Combining thousands of stations gives a model of plate velocities, such as the MORVEL model of DeMets and colleagues (2010), which fits the geological record averaged over the past few million years remarkably well.

Plates are not perfectly rigid, either. Near some boundaries, like the Himalaya and the western United States, the deformation spreads over wide zones hundreds of kilometres across rather than a single sharp line. This is why scientists talk about "plate boundary zones" as well as boundaries.

Another unsolved detail: why did plate tectonics start, and when? Evidence puts it somewhere between about 3 and 1 billion years ago, but the exact timing is still debated. Earth is the only planet in our solar system known to have it.
