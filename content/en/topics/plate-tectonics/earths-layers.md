The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, reached about 12 km. Earth's radius is about 6,371 km, so humanity has scratched less than 0.2% of the way to the centre. Everything we know about the layers below comes from indirect evidence, mostly from the way earthquake waves travel through the planet.

## Four main layers by composition

{{diagram:earth-layers}}

**Crust.** The thin, rocky outer skin. Under the oceans it is only about 5–10 km thick and made mostly of dark, dense basalt. Under continents it averages about 30–50 km (up to roughly 70 km under the highest mountain ranges) and is made of lighter, more varied rock such as granite. If Earth were an apple, the crust would be thinner than the skin.

**Mantle.** A layer of silicate rock about 2,890 km thick, making up around 84% of Earth's volume. It is **solid**, yet over millions of years it flows like extremely stiff putty, at a few centimetres per year. That slow churning is called mantle convection.

**Outer core.** A layer of **liquid** iron–nickel alloy with some lighter elements, from about 2,900 km to 5,150 km deep. Its motion generates Earth's magnetic field.

**Inner core.** A ball of solid, iron-rich metal with a radius of about 1,220 km. It is far hotter than the surface, yet it is solid because the pressure there is millions of times atmospheric pressure, which forces the atoms to stay locked in a crystal structure.

> **Common misconception.** "The inside of the Earth is molten lava." Only the outer core is liquid, and it is metal, not rock. The mantle, which supplies volcanoes, is solid rock that *creeps*. Magma forms only in special places where the rock partly melts (see [earthquakes and volcanoes](../earthquakes-and-volcanoes/)).

## Two more layers by strength

Plate tectonics depends on how rock *behaves*, not only what it is made of. Geologists therefore also divide the outer Earth by strength:

{{diagram:lithosphere-asthenosphere}}

- The **lithosphere** is the cold, rigid outer shell: the crust plus the uppermost, stiff part of the mantle. It is typically around 100 km thick, thinner at ocean ridges and thicker under old continents. It is brittle, so it breaks into **plates**.
- The **asthenosphere** lies below it. It is hot enough, and under low enough pressure, that it is weak and can flow slowly, so the plates can move over it. It is still mostly solid rock.

## How do we know? Listening to earthquakes

When an earthquake happens, it sends out two main kinds of waves that travel through the whole planet:

- **P-waves** (primary): push–pull waves, like sound. They travel through solids *and* liquids and are the fastest.
- **S-waves** (secondary): side-to-side shear waves. They can travel **only through solids**, because a liquid cannot resist a sideways shove.

Waves speed up, slow down, bend and reflect as they cross different materials. Seismometers around the world record when waves arrive, and scientists work backwards to find what they passed through. Two famous results:

1. **The outer core is liquid.** S-waves do not arrive on the far side of the planet at more than about 103° from the earthquake, and P-waves are bent so strongly that they leave a shadow zone. Both are explained by a liquid layer deep inside. Richard Oldham first noticed the core in 1906; Beno Gutenberg located its boundary in 1912–14.
2. **The inner core is solid.** In 1936 Inge Lehmann noticed faint P-waves arriving *inside* the shadow zone and realised they had been bent by a different, solid centre.

<summary>Go deeper: the Earth model and the Moho</summary>

Seismologists combine thousands of observations into a one-dimensional reference model. The best known is **PREM** (the Preliminary Reference Earth Model, 1981), which gives wave speeds and density at every depth. It shows sharp jumps at:

- the **Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho)**, the base of the crust, where P-wave speed jumps from roughly 6.8 to 8 km/s (discovered by Andrija Mohorovičić in 1909);
- the **transition zone** near 410 and 660 km depth, where minerals rearrange into denser crystal structures without any change in chemistry;
- the **core–mantle boundary** at 2,891 km, where P-wave speed drops from about 13.7 to 8 km/s and S-wave speed drops to zero;
- the **inner-core boundary** at 5,150 km.

Three-dimensional versions of this picture (seismic tomography) show that the mantle is not uniform. Cold slabs of old sea floor sinking into the mantle appear as fast regions, and two huge hot, slow regions sit under Africa and the Pacific.

The geometric P-wave shadow zone in this simple model starts at about 98°. Textbooks often quote 104° because weak, diffracted P-waves persist a few degrees further.
