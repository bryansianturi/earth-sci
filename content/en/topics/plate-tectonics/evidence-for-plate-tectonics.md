In 1912 the German meteorologist **Alfred Wegener** proposed that the continents had once been joined in a single supercontinent (**Pangaea**) and had since drifted apart. Most geologists rejected the idea for decades. Plate tectonics became the accepted theory only in the 1960s, once new evidence from the sea floor arrived. It is worth knowing both the old evidence and the new, because together they show how science builds confidence.

## Wegener's evidence (1910s–1930s)

1. **The fit of the continents.** The coasts of South America and Africa look like jigsaw pieces. The fit is better still if you match the edges of the continental shelves rather than today's coastlines (a computer fit by Bullard and colleagues in 1965 confirmed this).
2. **Matching fossils.** The same land-dwelling fossils appear on continents now separated by oceans: the freshwater reptile *Mesosaurus* in South America and southern Africa; the seed fern *Glossopteris* across South America, Africa, India, Antarctica and Australia. These organisms could not have crossed thousands of kilometres of open ocean.
3. **Matching rocks and mountains.** Mountain belts and rock layers line up across oceans, for example the Appalachians in North America with ranges in Scotland, Ireland and Scandinavia.
4. **Matching ancient climate.** About 300 million years ago ice sheets left scratched rocks and glacial deposits in South America, Africa, India and Australia, regions that today are tropical or temperate. Coal beds of tropical swamp forests lie near the poles. Moving the continents explains both.

Wegener's weakness was the **mechanism**. He suggested continents ploughed through the ocean floor, driven by weak forces such as Earth's rotation and tides. Physicists showed that was impossible. His central idea, that the continents move, was right; his proposed cause was wrong.

## The ocean floor evidence (1950s–1960s)

After World War II, ships mapped the ocean floor with sonar and measured its magnetism. They found surprises.

- **Mid-ocean ridges** form a continuous mountain chain about 65,000 km long, with a central valley. Earthquakes and volcanism are common along the ridge crest.
- **Sea-floor spreading.** In 1962 Harry Hess proposed that new crust forms at the ridges and moves away, with old crust consumed in the trenches, so the sea floor is a slowly moving conveyor.
- **Magnetic stripes.** Magnetic minerals in cooling lava line up with Earth's magnetic field and are locked in. Earth's field reverses polarity at irregular intervals (hundreds of thousands to millions of years apart). Ships towing magnetometers found that the sea floor has **alternating stripes** of stronger and weaker magnetism, parallel to the ridge and **symmetrical on both sides**. In 1963 Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews showed this is exactly what spreading plus magnetic reversals would produce.

- **Age of the sea floor.** Drilling and dating show that rock is youngest at the ridge and gets steadily older away from it. The oldest ocean floor still in place is only about 180 million years old (a few small remnants are older), whereas continental rocks go back up to about 4 billion years. The sea floor is geologically young because it is constantly recycled at subduction zones.

## More evidence that ties it together

- **Earthquakes and volcanoes** are not scattered randomly. They trace narrow belts that outline the plates. At subduction zones, earthquake foci form a dipping plane reaching as deep as about 700 km, the Wadati–Benioff zone, mapping the sinking slab (see [earthquakes and volcanoes](../earthquakes-and-volcanoes/)).
- **Hotspot tracks.** Chains of volcanic islands such as Hawaii get older in one direction, as a plate moves over a stationary source of melt in the mantle.
- **Paleomagnetism of continents.** Ancient rocks record the latitude where they formed. The record shows continents moving across latitudes over time.
- **GPS and satellite laser ranging.** Since the 1980s we can directly measure continents moving: for example North America and Europe are separating by roughly 2 cm per year, matching the rates deduced from the magnetic stripes.

> **Common misconception.** "Wegener was ignored because geologists were stubborn." Some were, but the main scientific objection was fair: he had no workable mechanism and some of his numbers were wrong. Acceptance came when independent new evidence (sea-floor spreading and magnetic stripes) supplied what was missing.

> **Common misconception.** "Continents plough through the ocean floor." No. The continents are embedded in plates and move *with* the ocean floor next to them.

<summary>Go deeper: how the stripes are dated</summary>

The reversals form a pattern that is the same in every ocean, like a barcode. Using lavas on land that can be dated with radioactive decay (potassium–argon and later argon–argon methods), geologists built a timescale of reversals (the geomagnetic polarity timescale). Once the barcode is calibrated, a stripe's position gives the age of the sea floor, and the distance from the ridge divided by the age gives the **spreading rate**.

This also explains the width differences: fast-spreading ridges like the East Pacific Rise produce wide stripes and a smooth ridge, whereas slow-spreading ridges like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge produce narrow stripes and a rugged, deep rift valley.

In the diagram, widths are schematic. Real stripes vary a lot because reversals happen at irregular intervals, which is exactly why the pattern is distinctive enough to date things.
